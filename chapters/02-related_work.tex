\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:relatedWork}

\section{Traditional Fact-Checking}

Traditional fact-checking refers to the manual verification of claims by journalists, researchers, and professional fact-checking organizations. This process involves investigating claims, consulting reliable sources, evaluating evidence, and publishing verification reports. Such systems play an important role in combating misinformation by providing carefully reviewed assessments of public claims and viral content ~\cite{QuestToAutomateFactChecking2015Oct}.

Despite its effectiveness, manual fact-checking faces several limitations. It is time-intensive, making it difficult to respond before misinformation spreads widely online. Furthermore, the volume of content generated through social media and digital platforms far exceeds the capacity of human fact-checkers, limiting scalability. Fact-checks are often published in unstructured formats across independent platforms, reducing their accessibility and reuse for automated systems. In addition, fact-checking organizations frequently face challenges related to audience reach, resource constraints, and perceived bias ~\cite{QuestToAutomateFactChecking2015Oct,FactChecking2025Mar}.

These limitations have motivated the development of automated fact-checking systems that leverage advances in natural language processing, information retrieval, and machine learning to perform verification at scale and with greater speed.

\section{Automated Fact-Checking}

Automated fact-checking aims to develop systems that can verify the accuracy of claims with minimal human intervention. Often described as the “Holy Grail” of computational journalism, such systems are expected to operate efficiently while providing accurate and transparent evidence-based verdicts ~\cite{QuestToAutomateFactChecking2015Oct}.

The task typically involves retrieving relevant evidence from large information sources and reasoning over the relationship between the claim and the retrieved evidence to determine its veracity. The rapid growth of online content and the increasing spread of misinformation have further motivated the development of scalable automated verification systems.

Early research focused on the creation of benchmark datasets for claim verification. For example, ~\citet{Vlachos2014Jun} introduced a dataset of 106 claims collected from fact-checking platforms such as PolitiFact. Although the dataset included verdict labels and journalistic justifications, the supporting evidence was not available in a machine-readable format, limiting its applicability to fully automated fact-checking systems.

Later, ~\citet{Wang2017Jul} expanded this line of research using approximately 12.8K claims collected through the PolitiFact API. However, due to the lack of structured evidence representations, the study relied solely on claim text and speaker metadata for classification, without retrieving or reasoning over supporting evidence. As a result, the system could predict verdicts but could not provide evidence-based justifications.

The Fake News Challenge approached fact-checking as a stance detection task, where the objective was to determine whether an article supported, refuted, discussed, or was unrelated to a given claim. Proposed by ~\citet{Ferreira2016Jun} and later formalized in the Fake News Challenge dataset, the task consisted of approximately 50K labeled claim–article pairs derived from 300 claims and 2,595 articles ~\cite{PomerleauDelip2017Jun}. Since the relevant articles were provided in advance, the task focused on claim classification rather than evidence retrieval.

A major advancement in automated fact-checking came with the introduction of \textit{FEVER} (Fact Extraction and VERification) by ~\citet{Thorne2018Jun}. The dataset consists of 185,445 claims verified against Wikipedia and established a retrieval-based fact-checking framework in which systems must first retrieve relevant evidence before predicting claim veracity.

FEVER defines three verdict labels: SUPPORTED, REFUTED, and NOT ENOUGH INFO. In addition to predicting the correct label, systems are required to provide sentence-level evidence supporting their decision. Many claims further require multi-hop reasoning, where evidence must be aggregated across multiple sentences or documents. By jointly evaluating evidence retrieval and claim verification, FEVER became a foundational benchmark for automated fact-checking research.

Despite its impact, FEVER has several limitations. The claims were synthetically generated from Wikipedia sentences and therefore do not fully reflect the complexity and diversity of real-world misinformation.

To address the limitations of FEVER, ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2023May} introduced \textit{AVeriTeC}, a fact-checking benchmark containing 4,568 real-world claims collected from professional fact-checking organizations. Unlike initial FEVER, AVeriTeC incorporates evidence retrieved from the open web, structured question–answer-based evidence collection, and human-written justifications explaining how the evidence supports or refutes a claim. The dataset was specifically designed to reduce common issues such as context dependence, insufficient evidence, and temporal leakage, making it more representative of real-world fact-checking scenarios.

However, both FEVER and AVeriTeC primarily focused on textual verification, whereas real-world misinformation frequently involves multimodal content such as images, videos, and screenshots. As a result, recent research has increasingly focused on multimodal fact-checking systems capable of jointly analyzing textual and visual information.

\section{Multimodal Fact Checking}

Recent studies estimate that nearly 80\% of online claims are multimodal, combining textual and visual content ~\cite{Dufour2024May}. Among these, images are the most frequently used medium for misinformation. Consequently, effective verification requires systems that can reason not only over textual and visual evidence independently but also over the relationship between the two modalities.

To support research in this area, ~\citet{Cao2025May} introduced AVerImaTeC(Automatic Verification of Image-Text Claims) at the FEVER 9 workshop. The dataset contains 1,297 real-world multimodal claims consisting of both images and text. Each claim is accompanied by web-retrieved question–answer evidence pairs, metadata, veracity labels, and human-written justifications explaining the final verdict.

\section{AVerImaTeC  Competition Systems Overview}

Recent multimodal fact-checking systems increasingly adopted Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architectures that combined evidence retrieval with multimodal reasoning. Using the AVerImaTeC dataset, ~\citet{Cao2025May} proposed a baseline framework consisting of three components: question generation, evidence retrieval, and claim verification. The system generates verification questions from the claim, retrieves supporting evidence from textual and visual sources, and predicts claim veracity using the retrieved evidence.

Building upon this baseline, the systems developed for the AVerImaTeC shared task explored a variety of retrieval, reasoning, and evidence aggregation strategies. Despite their architectural differences, these approaches share a common objective: to gather relevant textual and visual evidence before synthesizing a final verdict and justification using Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs).

~\citet{Tariq2026Mar} proposed REVEAL (Retrieval-Enhanced Verification with Evidence Accumulation Loop) which emphasized iterative evidence gathering and reasoning. Instead of relying on a single retrieval step, it progressively refined the search process based on previously collected evidence. The system combined textual and visual retrieval strategies to improve evidence coverage and reduce the gap between claims and supporting or refuting evidence. Its key contribution was to maintain an evidence accumulation loop that enables more informed and robust verification decisions.

~\citet{Tsoneva2026Mar} proposed XxP, which treated multimodality as a controllable design variable. The framework decomposed claims into text-related and image-related questions and applied dedicated retrieval strategies to each. A key design choice was the reliance on textual evidence for final verdict generation, based on the hypothesis that textual grounding provides more reliable veracity assessment.

~\citet{Upravitelev2026Mar} proposed ADA-AGGR, which emphasized breadth in evidence gathering by executing textual, visual, and multimodal retrieval strategies in parallel. The framework prioritized operational efficiency through aggressive visual downsampling while maintaining competitive verification performance.

~\citet{Ullrich2026Mar} proposed AIC CTU, a modular and cost-effective RAG-based system that combined offline textual retrieval with real-time reverse image search. The framework prioritized simplicity, reproducibility, and low computational overhead while achieving competitive performance.

~\citet{Jung2026Mar} proposed VILLAIN, a multi-agent framework in which specialized vision-language agents performed textual, visual, and cross-modal evidence analysis. The system further enriched its evidence base through web crawling and resolved inconsistencies using dedicated modality-specific reasoning agents.

\section{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Fact-Checking}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in natural language understanding and reasoning, leading to their adoption in automated fact-checking systems. However, several studies have highlighted limitations that reduce their reliability for factual verification ~\cite{Proma2025Feb,Lewis2020May}.

A key challenge is the lack of reliable grounding in external evidence. When asked to justify their predictions, LLMs may generate fabricated facts, sources, or citations. Furthermore, because their knowledge is limited to the data available during training, they often struggle to verify recent events or rapidly evolving information. These limitations are compounded by the tendency of LLMs to produce incorrect information with high confidence, making ungrounded predictions difficult to detect.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) reduced reliance on a model's internal parametric knowledge by grounding generation in externally retrieved evidence ~\cite{Lewis2020May}. This evidence-centric paradigm has become a cornerstone of modern fact-checking systems, particularly in FEVER-style tasks where relevant evidence must be retrieved before claim verification.

~\citet{Lewis2020May} demonstrated the effectiveness of RAG on the FEVER benchmark by retrieving evidence from Wikipedia and reasoning over the retrieved information to predict claim veracity. Despite using a relatively simple retrieval architecture, the approach achieved performance within 4.3\% of state-of-the-art systems on the FEVER three-way classification task.

Subsequent fact-checking frameworks have widely adopted RAG-based architectures. The FEVER baselines proposed by ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2023May} and ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2024Nov} combined evidence retrieval from search engines and offline knowledge stores with transformer-based reasoning models. Similarly, all leading systems in the AVerImaTeC shared task employed RAG as the core framework for integrating evidence retrieval, reasoning, and verdict generation ~\cite{Cao2026Mar}.

\section{Text Retrieval System}

\subsection{Sparse Retrieval}

Sparse retrieval is an information retrieval technique that identifies relevant documents through exact matching of query terms and document tokens using statistical word-frequency information. Due to their efficiency and scalability, they have been widely adopted as first-stage retrievers in large-scale information retrieval systems ~\cite{Wang2022Aug}.

TF–IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) represents one of the earliest and most fundamental sparse retrieval methods. It scores documents based on the importance of query terms within a document and their rarity across the entire document collection ~\cite{Wang2024Jul}.

BM25 (Best Matching 25) extends traditional TF–IDF by introducing a non-linear term weighting scheme and document length normalization, resulting in more effective relevance scoring ~\cite{Robertson2009Sep}. 

BM25 is especially effective at matching rare phrases, keywords, and specific entity names. For example, it can accurately retrieve passages containing unique terms such as “Thoros of Myr,” which dense encoders may fail to represent precisely ~\cite{Karpukhin2020Nov}. Due to these strengths, BM25 has been widely adopted in fact-checking systems, including FEVER competition baselines by ~\citet{Cao2025May}, the ADA-AGGR framework proposed by ~\citet{Upravitelev2026Mar}, Xxp by ~\citet{Tsoneva2026Mar} and REVEAL proposed by ~\citet{Tariq2026Mar}. These systems use BM25 as a "first-pass" filter to narrow down large knowledge bases into a manageable set of candidates for more expensive dense retrievers to analyze.

~\citet{Won2025Nov} described the main limitation of BM25 is its dependence on exact keyword matching, which restricts its ability to capture deeper semantic relationships in complex queries. Although computationally efficient, BM25 generally underperforms compared to modern neural sparse retrieval models in large-scale retrieval tasks in metrics like MRR@10 and Semantic Similarity Scores (SSS). SPLADE addresses these limitations through learned sparse lexical representations and query expansion, enabling improved semantic understanding and retrieval performance. However, SPLADE introduces higher computational cost and latency compared to BM25, especially for short queries, and remains constrained by the fixed BERT WordPiece vocabulary. Due to its ability to combine lexical precision with semantic expansion, SPLADE has been adopted in recent fact-checking systems, including Team fv (Factiverse), where it is used for text related evidence retrieval.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval}

Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) introduced a shift from traditional sparse retrieval methods such as BM25 and SPLADE toward dense dual-encoder architectures. Instead of relying on exact keyword matching, DPR uses BERT-based encoders to map queries and documents into a shared dense embedding space, enabling retrieval based on semantic similarity. This allows the model to capture latent semantic relationships and lexical variations, such as associating “bad guy” with “villain,” which sparse retrieval methods may fail to identify ~\citep{Karpukhin2020Nov}.

Empirically, DPR significantly outperformed BM25, improving top-20 retrieval accuracy by 9–19\% across several benchmarks ~\citep{Karpukhin2020Nov}. As a result, dense retrieval has become a core component of modern Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and fact-checking systems, where it is often combined with sparse retrieval methods to leverage the strengths of both approaches ~\cite{Schlichtkrull2024Nov}. Recent multimodal fact-checking frameworks, including AIC CTU ~\cite{Ullrich2026Mar} and VILLAIN ~\cite{Jung2026Mar}, employ dense embedding models such as \textit{mxbai-embed-large-v1} for semantic retrieval of textual evidence.

\subsection{Hybrid Retrieval}

~\citet{Chen2022Jan} and ~\citet{Gao2020Apr} highlight the complementary strengths of sparse and dense retrieval methods. Dense retrievers excel at semantic matching but may fail to capture exact keywords, entity names, or technical terms, and often generalize poorly under significant domain shifts. In contrast, sparse retrieval methods are robust to domain variation and effective at exact lexical matching, but struggle to capture deeper semantic relationships between queries and documents.

To address these limitations, hybrid retrieval combines sparse and dense representations, leveraging the lexical precision of sparse methods and the semantic understanding of dense retrievers. Empirical studies show that hybrid retrieval consistently outperforms either approach individually, improving retrieval accuracy, cross-domain robustness, and long-document retrieval performance ~\cite{Gao2020Apr} ~\cite{Chen2022Jan}~\cite{Luan2021}. Consequently, hybrid retrieval has become a common strategy in modern Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems and fact-checking pipelines.

\subsection{Fusion Between Text Retrieval sources}
Although sparse and dense retrieval methods provide complementary strengths, combining them effectively remains challenging due to differences in their score distributions. Dense retrieval scores are typically normalized, whereas sparse retrieval scores can vary substantially in magnitude, making direct score fusion difficult.

Many hybrid retrieval approaches address this problem through linear interpolation of sparse and dense retrieval scores. However, such methods are sensitive to score scaling and fusion weights, often requiring dataset-specific normalization and tuning. Furthermore, score distributions may vary across domains, limiting the robustness of these approaches ~\cite{Wang2021Jul}.

To overcome these limitations, ~\citet{Chen2022Jan} performed sparse and dense retrieval independently and combined the resulting rankings using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). Rather than aggregating raw scores, RRF ranks documents based on their positions across multiple retrieval models. This approach has been shown to produce robust retrieval ensembles while avoiding domain-specific score normalization and weight tuning, enabling effective zero-shot generalization to new domains.

Rather than performing sparse and dense retrieval independently and combining the results using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), ~\citet{Zhang2024Oct} proposed unified hybrid indexing approaches that store dense and sparse representations within a single index. This is typically achieved by concatenating dense and sparse vectors into a unified representation and performing retrieval using algorithms such as Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) or Inverted File (IVF) indexing.

However, unified indexing introduced significant computational challenges. Dense and sparse vectors exhibit substantially different distributions, while sparse vectors are highly dimensional and computationally expensive to process. As a result, sparse distance calculations can account for over 60\% of the total search time in graph-based hybrid indexes and may require up to 11 times more computation than dense similarity calculations ~\cite{Zhang2024Oct}.

REVEAL employed a weighted multi-query retrieval strategy that executed four query types in parallel, including debunking heuristics, primary questions, claim context, and query augmentation. The results were aggregated using a weighted variant of Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), allowing higher-priority query types to contribute more strongly to the final ranking. This design enabled evidence matching debunking-oriented queries to be prioritized even when their individual retrieval scores were lower ~\cite{Tariq2026Mar}.

\section{Multimodal Retrieval}

Early research in automated fact-checking primarily focused on textual claims and textual evidence. However, the growing prevalence of multimodal misinformation has highlighted the need for systems capable of jointly processing textual and visual information. Studies have shown that combining text with images can significantly increase the perceived credibility and spread of false claims, motivating the development of multimodal fact-checking approaches that incorporate visual evidence alongside textual reasoning ~\cite{Hameleers2020Feb}.


Early multimodal fact-checking approaches, such as the work by ~\citet{Xu2024Mar}, utilized CLIP  (Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training) to jointly encode textual and visual information into a shared feature space. The extracted image and text representations were fused using attention mechanisms and bilinear pooling, followed by a classifier to predict claim veracity.

CLIP is a multimodal model that learns a shared embedding space for images and text, enabling cross-modal retrieval through semantic similarity matching ~\cite{Radford2021Feb}. Due to its strong zero-shot capabilities, CLIP has become a foundational model for multimodal retrieval and has been adopted in fact-checking systems such as the AVerImaTeC baseline and the Team Factiverse framework ~\cite{Cao2026Mar}. However, its text encoder is limited to 77 input tokens, motivating the development of models such as LongCLIP for longer textual contexts.

LongCLIP addresses the limitations of CLIP by extending the maximum text input length from 77 to 248 tokens while preserving the strengths of the original model. This enables more effective alignment between images and longer textual descriptions, improving retrieval performance for descriptive evidence and image-associated narratives commonly encountered in fact-checking tasks. As a result, LongCLIP achieves substantial improvements in long-caption image–text retrieval while also improving performance on traditional benchmarks such as COCO and Flickr30k ~\cite{Zhang2024Mar}.

Beyond image–text retrieval, ~\citet{Samuel2025Jul} employed SigLIP (Sigmoid Loss for Language–Image Pre-training) to bridge the modality gap between textual queries and visual content. Unlike CLIP, SigLIP replaces softmax normalization with a sigmoid-based loss function, improving training scalability and retrieval performance. Using SigLIP for multimodal retrieval, the authors reported significant improvements on the MultiVENT 2.0 dataset, achieving an 81\% increase in nDCG@20 compared to leading multimodal encoders and a 37\% improvement over single-modality retrieval systems.

Recent multimodal fact-checking systems have adopted a variety of image retrieval models. REVEAL employed SigLIP2-Large to construct a shared visual embedding space for semantic image retrieval ~\cite{Tariq2026Mar}. Similarly, the XxP framework used SigLIP to jointly encode textual queries and claim images for retrieval from an image-related knowledge store ~\cite{Tsoneva2026Mar}. ADA-AGGR utilized ColPali 1.3, which leverages late-interaction mechanisms to match textual queries against multimodal image representations, supporting both image-only and multimodal retrieval ~\cite{Upravitelev2026Mar}. In contrast, VILLAIN employed the Ops-MM-embedding-v1-7B model to retrieve visual evidence using both claim text and claim images ~\cite{Jung2026Mar}. These approaches demonstrate the growing importance of multimodal embedding models for retrieving visually grounded evidence in modern fact-checking systems.

\section{Evidence Ranking and Diversification}

Cross-encoders are widely used for reranking because they jointly process query–document pairs, enabling more accurate relevance estimation than bi-encoder methods. While computationally expensive, they are particularly effective in zero-shot and out-of-domain retrieval scenarios ~\cite{Rosa2022Dec}.

~\citet{Rosa2022Dec} employed cross-encoders such as monoT5 as second-stage rerankers to reorder the top 1,000 candidate documents retrieved by BM25. Their study showed that reranking performance remained consistently strong regardless of whether the initial candidates were retrieved using sparse methods like BM25 or dense retrievers such as GTR (Generalizable T5-based dense Retrievers).

Similarly ~\citet{Ma2023May} used LLMs like GPT-3 as rerankers  where it takes query and a list of multiple documents simultaneously and generate a reordered list of document identifiers based on their relevance.

Recent multimodal fact-checking systems commonly employ a second-stage reranking component to refine the initial retrieval results. REVEAL used SFR-Embedding-2\_R to rerank candidates retrieved by sparse search ~\cite{Tariq2026Mar}, while XxP, ADA-AGGR, and VILLAIN applied dense embedding models such as gte-base, Qwen3-Embedding, and mxbai-rerank-large-v1, respectively, to improve evidence ranking and filter irrelevant documents ~\cite{Tsoneva2026Mar,Upravitelev2026Mar,Jung2026Mar}. These systems demonstrate the importance of reranking as an effective mechanism for improving retrieval quality before downstream fact verification.

Although rerankers improve retrieval accuracy, they often assign high relevance scores to duplicate or highly similar documents, reducing evidence diversity. To address this limitation, ~\citet{Carbonell1998Aug} introduced Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR), a diversity-aware reranking method that balances document relevance against redundancy. By prioritizing evidence that contributes new information, MMR promotes broader evidence coverage and more informative retrieval results. The balance between relevance and diversity is controlled by a tunable parameter ($\lambda$), allowing systems to favor either highly relevant or more diverse evidence sets ~\cite{Ullrich2024Nov}.


MMR has also been adopted in recent fact-checking systems. For example, AIC CTU applied MMR to rerank the top 20 retrieved candidates into a final set of seven evidence documents. The system used a trade-off parameter of \(\lambda = 0.8\), favoring relevance while still promoting evidence diversity ~\cite{Ullrich2026Mar}.


\section{LLM and MLLM for Fact-Checking}

Another key component of a fact-checking system is the reasoning module, which analyzes retrieved evidence to determine the veracity of a claim. Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly employed for this task due to their ability to reason over complex textual and visual information. Beyond factual knowledge, these models can evaluate plausibility, narrative structure, and linguistic style, enabling effective performance even on recent or opinion-based claims. However, their performance varies across domains, languages, and claim types, and they often struggle more with factual-sounding claims than subjective statements ~\cite{Tang2024Nov}.

LLMs play a central role in modern fact-checking systems by generating verification questions, interpreting retrieved evidence, and reasoning over the gathered information to predict claim veracity. Recent frameworks such as those proposed by ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2023May}, ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2024Nov}, ~\citet{Ullrich2024Nov}, and ~\citet{Ullrich2025Aug} have successfully leveraged LLMs for evidence analysis and verdict generation.

Following the introduction of the multimodal AVerImaTeC dataset ~\cite{Cao2025May}, most competitive fact-checking systems adopted Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to reason over both claims and retrieved multimodal evidence ~\cite{Cao2026Mar}. Proprietary models such as Gemini-2.5-Pro, Gemini 3 Pro, and GPT-5.1 were employed by systems including VILLAIN, ADA-AGGR, and AIC CTU, respectively ~\cite{Jung2026Mar,Upravitelev2026Mar,Ullrich2026Mar}. In contrast, open-weight models such as Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-VL-32B were adopted by XxP, REVEAL, and Team Factiverse ~\cite{Tsoneva2026Mar,Tariq2026Mar}. The AVerImaTeC baseline similarly evaluated a range of LLMs and MLLMs, including Gemini, Qwen-VL, Gemma, and LLaVA variants ~\cite{Cao2025May}. These results highlight the central role of MLLMs as reasoning engines in modern multimodal fact-checking systems.

\section{QA generation in fact checking}

Question–Answer (QA) pairs play an important role in fact-checking by decomposing complex claims into smaller, verifiable sub-questions, thereby improving evidence retrieval and verification. ~\citet{Setty2024Jul} demonstrated that automated question decomposition can be effectively performed using smaller fine-tuned generative models, achieving up to 8\% higher question generation performance than large language models. Their study also showed that evidence retrieved using machine-generated questions was more effective for fact-checking than evidence retrieved using human-written questions.

In automated fact-checking systems such as Varifocal, ~\citet{Ousidhoum2022Dec} generated verification questions by first extracting claim-specific focal points using dependency parsing and then producing targeted questions for each focal point. A subsequent reranking stage was used to remove duplicates and retain the most informative questions for evidence retrieval and verification.

The AVerImaTeC baseline study by ~\citet{Cao2025May} explored three question-generation strategies: Parallel Question Generation (PQG), which generates all questions simultaneously; Dynamic Question Generation (DQG), where questions are conditioned on previously retrieved evidence; and Hybrid Question Answering (HQA), which combines both approaches to balance efficiency and adaptivity during verification.


Recent multimodal fact-checking systems employ diverse question-generation strategies. XxP  generated multiple verification questions (typically 3–5) and classify them as either text-related or image-related, enabling modality-aware evidence retrieval ~\cite{Tsoneva2026Mar}. Team Factiverse and REVEAL extended this approach with an iterative, history-aware strategy in which each new question is generated based on previously retrieved evidence, improving adaptability at the cost of increased computational complexity ~\cite{Tariq2026Mar}.

In contrast, ADA-AGGR generates a single verification question from the claim text and associated images, which is then used to retrieve textual and visual evidence for downstream reasoning ~\cite{Upravitelev2026Mar}. AIC CTU avoids a dedicated question-generation stage by first retrieving evidence and then prompting the MLLM to generate question–answer pairs directly from the retrieved content before predicting claim veracity ~\cite{Ullrich2026Mar}.

VILLAIN adopts a multi-agent reasoning framework, where specialized agents analyze textual, visual, and cross-modal evidence before generating diagnostic question–answer pairs that support the final verdict ~\cite{Jung2026Mar}. These approaches illustrate the trade-offs between retrieval depth, interpretability, computational cost, and reasoning complexity in modern fact-checking systems.

Several recent fact-checking systems employ in-context learning (ICL) by retrieving similar training examples using BM25 and providing them as few-shot demonstrations to the language model. Frameworks such as VILLAIN, AIC CTU, and the AVerImaTeC baseline use this strategy to improve question generation and evidence-based reasoning ~\cite{Jung2026Mar,Ullrich2026Mar,Cao2025May}.


Despite advances in reasoning and prompting strategies, retrieved evidence remains the foundation of modern fact-checking systems. In multimodal fact-checking, the quality of retrieved textual and visual evidence directly affects downstream question generation, reasoning, and verdict prediction. Consequently, robust multimodal retrieval remains one of the most critical challenges in automated fact verification.

\section{Evaluation of Fact-Checking Systems}

Robust evaluation methods are essential for assessing the accuracy and generalizability of fact-checking systems. Early evaluation approaches, such as those used in the FEVER Workshop 2021 ~\cite{Aly2021Nov}, relied on a strict metric that awarded credit only when a system correctly predicted both the claim verdict and at least one complete gold evidence set through exact evidence matching.

To address the limitations of exact matching, later works by ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2023May} and ~\citet{Schlichtkrull2024Nov} adopted Hungarian METEOR to evaluate the similarity between generated and reference question–answer evidence pairs. This metric supports approximate matching, allowing evidence expressed in different forms or sourced from different documents to receive partial credit. Furthermore, verdict predictions are only rewarded when the associated evidence exceeds a Hungarian METEOR threshold of 0.25, ensuring that predictions remain grounded in sufficiently relevant evidence.

More recently, the AVerImaTeC shared task introduced evaluation methods for multimodal evidence ~\cite{Cao2025May}. Generated evidence is compared against human-annotated references using an LLM-based evaluator (Gemini-2.0-Flash) in a two-stage process that assesses both textual and visual similarity. In addition to evidence quality, generated justifications are evaluated using ROUGE-1 and Ev2R, a metric shown to correlate well with human judgments in open-ended generation tasks.

\section{Research Gap}

Existing multimodal fact-checking systems increasingly rely on retrieval-augmented generation pipelines that combine evidence retrieval with large language or vision-language models. However, evidence retrieval remains a major challenge, particularly when relevant information is distributed across textual and visual modalities. While recent AVerImaTeC systems have explored various retrieval and reasoning architectures, there is limited understanding of how different retrieval modalities, retrieval fusion strategies, and question-answer generation approaches contribute to downstream fact-checking performance.

This thesis addresses this gap by investigating a hybrid multimodal retrieval pipeline that combines dense semantic retrieval, sparse lexical retrieval, and image-text retrieval. In addition, the work compares iterative and single-pass question-answer generation strategies and evaluates the contribution of individual retrieval and post-retrieval components through systematic ablation studies. The proposed approach is evaluated on the AVerImaTeC benchmark to analyze the impact of retrieval design choices on evidence quality, verdict prediction, and justification generation.
